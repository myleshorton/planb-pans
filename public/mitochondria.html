Field Guide · Deconstructed

Mitochondrial dysfunction.
The energy layer.

The piece that explains the question every PANS parent asks: why does my kid crash after exertion or illness — and not bounce back? Mitochondria are the tiny power plants inside every cell that make the body’s energy. Under chronic stress — infection, mold, toxins, inflammation, the same drivers that fuel PANS — they stop making energy and flip into defense mode. That’s the energy layer: the fatigue, the crashes, the brain fog, the slow recovery. Here’s what it is, why it matters, how to test it, and how to support it gently — without pushing through.

If your child — or you — is running on empty no matter how much you rest, this layer may be why. You don’t have to find the way out alone.

First — what are mitochondria?

You learned the word in school: the “powerhouse of the cell.” Here’s what it actually means. Inside almost every cell are hundreds of tiny power plants called mitochondria. Their job is to take the food you eat and the oxygen you breathe and turn it into ATP — the single molecule the body burns for everything: thinking, moving, fighting infection, healing, holding emotions steady. The brain is the hungriest organ of all, so when energy production falters, the brain feels it first.

When mitochondria run well, the body has energy to think, heal, and stay regulated. When they flip to defense mode, the lights dim.

When they run well, the body has energy to spare. When they’re struggling, every system that needs power — the brain, the immune system, detox, mood regulation — runs short. That’s the energy layer of PANS.

What this targets

This addresses the energy / recovery engine — the layer that decides whether a child can tolerate exertion, fight off an illness, and bounce back afterward. When the mitochondria are the bottleneck, it shows up as fatigue, post-exertional crashes, brain fog, and a fuse that’s short because there’s no reserve. The honest caveat: only testing — OAT, CoQ10, carnitine, lactate/pyruvate, micronutrients read together — tells you whether this is actually your child’s layer, and the real fix is addressing the drivers underneath it.

Energy mode vs. defense mode — the Cell Danger Response

Here’s the idea that reframes everything. Mitochondria don’t only make energy — they’re also the cell’s threat sensors. When they detect danger — an infection, a toxin, mold, sustained inflammation — they do something protective: they stop prioritizing energy production and switch into defense. They harden the cell, send out alarm signals, and pull back from normal metabolism to protect the body while the threat is handled.

Dr. Robert Naviaux (UC San Diego) named this the Cell Danger Response (CDR). It’s a brilliant, healthy survival reflex — the problem is when it gets stuck on. If the drivers never fully clear (a smoldering infection, ongoing mold exposure, chronic inflammation), the mitochondria stay in danger mode long after the acute threat passes. The cell is braced for an emergency that never ends — so it never gets back to making enough energy. Naviaux, “Metabolic features of the cell danger response,” Mitochondrion, 2014.

That stuck-on state is the energy layer: fatigue, post-exertional crashes, brain fog, poor stress tolerance, and slow recovery. It’s not laziness, and it’s not “in their head” — it’s a measurable shift in how the cell’s power plants are running.

Why it matters for PANS

Every PANS driver hits the mitochondria. That’s the key point. Strep, Bartonella, Lyme, viruses, mold and its mycotoxins, heavy metals, chronic neuroinflammation — each one trips the danger sensor and pushes the cell toward defense mode. So mitochondrial dysfunction usually isn’t a separate problem; it’s the downstream toll of whatever is driving the PANS, sitting underneath the symptoms.

This is why kids crash after exertion or illness and don’t bounce back. A typical kid burns energy and refills the tank overnight. A kid in the stuck-on danger state has no reserve — so a hard day, a sports practice, or a passing cold spends energy the body can’t replace, and they crash, sometimes a day or two later. The slow recovery is the same story: the power plants are still in defense mode, not production mode.

Where it sits among the other layers

  • It ties straight to methylation. The methylation / detox engine needs energy to run, and the mitochondria need a clean cell to work in. When one is down, the other struggles — which is why so many of these kids are stuck in both at once.
  • It’s wrapped in oxidative stress. Mitochondria in defense mode throw off more free radicals, which burn through the body’s antioxidants (glutathione especially) — the same antioxidants methylation depends on. The two layers feed each other.
  • It explains the “tired but wired” kid — exhausted and depleted, yet unable to settle, because a dysregulated, under-powered nervous system can’t downshift.

And it doesn’t only hit the child. The same mechanism is part of why the PANS mom is so wiped out — running on years of chronic stress, broken sleep, and often her own infection or mold exposure, her mitochondria are in the same defense state. If this layer applies to your kid, it’s worth quietly asking whether it applies to you, too. You can’t pour from an empty cell.

Signs of the energy layer

None of these prove mitochondrial dysfunction on their own — but together, this pattern is the tell. Watch for:

  • Profound fatigue — tired in a way that rest doesn’t fully fix.
  • Post-exertional “crashes” — a hard day, exercise, or excitement is paid for with a slump, sometimes delayed by a day or two.
  • Brain fog — slow processing, word-finding trouble, “cotton-headed,” can’t hold a thought.
  • Low stamina / exercise intolerance — can’t keep up with peers, breathless or wiped out fast.
  • Slow recovery from illness — an ordinary cold knocks them flat for far longer than it should, and often flares the PANS.
  • Poor stress tolerance — a short fuse and big reactions to small things, because there’s no reserve to absorb the load.
  • “Tired but wired” — exhausted yet unable to wind down or sleep.

If this is your child — or your whole household — the energy layer is worth testing for directly. The point is never to push harder; it’s to find out whether the power plants are the bottleneck, and then to take the load off while you fix what’s underneath.

The testing

There’s no single mitochondrial test — you build a picture from a handful of markers and read them together. Here’s the full menu, grouped by sample type, with what each one tells you. A clean packet to print and bring to your practitioner; you don’t need every line at once.

①  Urine

  • Organic Acids Test (OAT) — the cornerstone. A urine test that reads the actual by-products of energy metabolism: the Krebs cycle (citric-acid-cycle) markers that show where the energy assembly line is stalling, fatty-acid-oxidation markers (whether the body can burn fat for fuel), and a CoQ10 marker. It also reads yeast, bacterial, and detox markers — the same OAT used in the methylation workup. Mosaic Diagnostics, mosaicdx.com; Genova also offers one.

②  Blood

  • CoQ10 (ubiquinone) — a core electron-transport-chain nutrient; low levels are common when mitochondria are struggling.
  • Carnitine — free and total — carnitine ferries fat into the mitochondria to be burned. Low free carnitine is a classic mitochondrial-strain marker.
  • Acylcarnitine profile — the more detailed companion to the carnitine panel; the pattern can point to where fatty-acid burning is jamming.
  • Lactate and pyruvate — with the lactate:pyruvate ratio — one of the most useful mitochondrial clues. When the energy chain backs up, lactate rises; the ratio (not either number alone) is what matters, so order both. (Sample handling matters — ask the lab how to draw it.)
  • CK (creatine kinase) — a muscle-energy enzyme; can be elevated when muscle cells are energy-stressed.
  • Comprehensive micronutrient panel — SpectraCell or Genova ION — the cofactors the energy chain can’t run without: B-vitamins, magnesium, CoQ10, carnitine, alpha-lipoic acid, antioxidants. It tells you exactly which fuel the engine is short on. SpectraCell, spectracell.com; Genova ION, gdx.net.

The honest catch — read it as a pattern

No one marker is a diagnosis. A single “low CoQ10” or a slightly high lactate isn’t the answer — the picture lives in the pattern across the OAT, the carnitine panel, the lactate:pyruvate ratio, and the micronutrient panel, read together and anchored to the symptoms. This cross-test synthesis is exactly what Minta does — and it’s the same OAT that feeds the methylation read, so one test does double duty.

Honest note: several of these are practitioner-ordered (the OAT, SpectraCell/ION, and acylcarnitine especially); a few have direct-to-consumer paths. Minta helps you know exactly what to ask for.

Support — gentle, test-anchored, and never “push through”

This is where the Plan B discipline matters most. Mitochondrial support is real and can help — but for a sensitive kid, more is not better, and the biggest lever isn’t a supplement at all. Here’s the honest frame.

Rule one — stop pushing through the crashes

Pacing and rest are the first intervention, before any supplement. If the tank is empty, demanding more output digs the hole deeper — the crash teaches you the ceiling, so respect it. Plan the day under the limit, build in recovery, and don’t “power through” a slump. This is the single most protective thing you can do, and it costs nothing.

Rule two — the real fix is upstream

Supplements support the engine; they don’t fix why it’s in defense mode. The mitochondria are stuck on because of the drivers — infection, mold, toxins, inflammation — and the methylation / detox engine they depend on. Address the upstream drivers and the energy layer recovers on its own. Mito support is the bridge that makes the kid more comfortable while you do the real work — not the destination.

What practitioners use — low and slow, confirm with labs first

These are the categories an integrative practitioner reaches for. We’re naming what’s used, not how much — no doses here on purpose. Each one should be anchored to a test (don’t supplement a nutrient you haven’t shown is low), started one at a time, low and slow, and run with your practitioner:

  • CoQ10 / ubiquinol — the electron-transport-chain workhorse; the ubiquinol form is better absorbed.
  • Carnitine — ferries fat into the mitochondria; paired with low free-carnitine on labs.
  • B-vitamins — the spark plugs of the energy cycle (B1/thiamine especially — see the methylation page on the missed B1 cofactor).
  • Magnesium — required for the body to actually make and use ATP; calming as a bonus.
  • Alpha-lipoic acid (ALA) — a mitochondrial antioxidant that also recycles others.
  • NAD+ / NR (nicotinamide riboside) — raw material for the energy chain.
  • D-ribose — a sugar the body uses to rebuild ATP; sometimes used for the post-exertional crash.
  • Creatine — an energy buffer for brain and muscle.

The frame: these are “what’s used, low and slow, confirm with labs first” — the same genetics/test-first, food-first discipline Plan B applies everywhere. A sensitive kid can react to any of these (over-methylators and the “tired but wired” kid especially), so one new thing at a time, watch the response, and let the labs — not a protocol off the internet — drive the choice.

Food first, here too. Before reaching for a stack, the gentlest support is the foundation: real food the body can convert to fuel, blood-sugar stability (no crash-and-spike), magnesium- and B-vitamin-rich foods, sleep protected fiercely, and gentle movement within the energy envelope — never beyond it. The mitochondria heal in a calm, well-fed, well-rested cell.

Bottom line

Mitochondrial dysfunction is the energy layer of PANS — the reason a kid crashes after exertion or illness and doesn’t bounce back. Every PANS driver trips the Cell Danger Response and pushes the cell’s power plants from energy mode into defense mode; when the drivers never clear, they get stuck there. The honest evidence: the CDR framework (Naviaux) is published and well-regarded, and the mitochondrial markers are measurable, but the broader treatment stack is clinical, not RCT-proven — which means: test it, support it gently, and respect the crashes. Don’t push through; fix what’s upstream. Parent education, not medical advice — bring it to your team as questions.
