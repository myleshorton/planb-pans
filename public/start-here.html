Four ideas, and you’ve got the whole picture

Everything in PANS comes down to four plain things: a blueprint, a maintenance crew, a set of batteries, and a fire. If you hold just these four, the rest of this guide makes sense. Here they are, one at a time.

1Genes are the blueprint

Your child’s genes are the blueprint they were born with. They tell you what the body might struggle with — like knowing a certain car model tends to have weak brakes. Useful to know. But the blueprint can’t tell you whether the brakes are actually worn right now. For that, you have to look at the real car.

The trap to avoid

Parents get a genetic test back, see a scary-looking gene (you’ll hear names like MTHFR), and think they finally have the answer. They don’t — they have the prediction, not the reality. The gene says “this part might run weak.” Only a test of how the body is actually running today can tell you if it really is. Never treat the blueprint. Treat the child.

2Methylation is the maintenance crew

Inside the body there’s a maintenance crew that works around the clock. It builds and repairs (including the brain chemicals that steady mood and focus), it calms inflammation, and it hauls out toxins. Its name is methylation — but you can just think of it as the crew.

When the crew keeps up, the body cleans up after itself and your kid feels steady. When the crew falls behind, everything slips at once — mood, focus, sleep, handling stress, and the ability to clear toxins out.

Here’s the part that matters most

Genes like MTHFR only tell you the crew might be short-staffed. They don’t tell you it actually is.

A simple blood test is how you check whether the crew is really behind today — the marker is called homocysteine. You support your child based on the blood, never the gene alone.

This is huge: lots of kids have the “bad” gene and a crew that’s keeping up just fine. Pushing supplements at a crew that’s already fine can actually make things worse. So: check the blood first, then help.

3Mitochondria are the batteries

In every single cell there are tiny power plants called mitochondria — think of them as the batteries that make all the energy your kid runs on. When the batteries are charged, your child can think clearly, recover from a hard day, and bounce back from getting sick.

Infection, mold, and inflammation drain the batteries. A kid with drained batteries crashes, can’t bounce back, feels foggy, and is exhausted in a way that sleep doesn’t fix.

The crew and the batteries are one system

Here’s the connection people miss: the maintenance crew (idea 2) literally builds parts for the batteries. So when methylation falls behind, the batteries run down too. They aren’t two separate problems to chase — they’re one engine. Fix the crew and you help the batteries. That’s why Plan B treats them together.

4The infection is what lit the fire

In PANS, the sudden change you saw in your child — the overnight OCD, the rage, the tics, the regression — was usually set off by an infection. Most often it’s strep; sometimes it’s Mycoplasma (a lung bug) or a virus. That infection is what lit the fire.

Find it early. Catching the trigger is often the fastest way to calm a flare — and you simply can’t fix what you haven’t found. So early on, a big part of the job is looking carefully for what lit the fire in the first place.

The heart of this page

The order to do things in

This is the part to really hold onto. Once you understand the four ideas, the order matters more than almost anything else. Do these things in the wrong order and a sensitive kid gets worse. Do them in this order and the body can actually handle the work. Here’s the sequence:

1

Day 1 — Understand + Find

Start by getting the picture: the genes (the blueprint), the blood (what’s actually happening right now), and the infection trigger (what lit the fire). You can’t fix what you haven’t found — so first you look.

2

Support the engine

Give the maintenance crew and the batteries what the blood says they actually need — and open the drains so the body can take out the trash. You can also put out an active fire here: a current strep or Mycoplasma infection gets treated now with standard, targeted treatment. Get the engine working before you ask it to do heavy lifting.

3

THEN the deep kill — last

Chronic, dug-in infections — things like Lyme and Bartonella — come last. Not because they don’t matter, but because clearing them creates a huge cleanup job. You wait until the engine (crew + batteries) can actually handle that cleanup.

The one distinction that prevents the most harm

There are two very different kinds of “killing,” and mixing them up is where families get hurt:

Putting out the active fire — treating a current, acute infection (a fresh strep or Mycoplasma) with standard targeted treatment. This is fine early. It’s a quick, contained job.

The long demolition — going after chronic, stealth infections like Lyme and Bartonella that have been dug in for a long time. This floods the body with die-off (dead bugs and the toxins they release), so it waits until the engine is ready. Same word, “kill” — completely different size of job.

Why this order — the picture that makes it stick

Killing infections always makes a mess — dead bugs and the toxins they release. That mess has to get hauled out by the maintenance crew, through the drains. If you start killing before the crew and the batteries are working, the mess backs up with nowhere to go — and that’s when the child crashes.

“You don’t demolish the house before the trash service is running.”

Get the trash service running first — support the engine, open the drains. Then you can start the demolition, and the body can carry the mess out instead of drowning in it.

We say this gently, because it’s the hardest lesson in PANS: starting a kill phase before the engine was ready is exactly what harms some kids. The treatment wasn’t “wrong” — the order was. That’s the whole reason this page exists, and the whole reason the order above comes before anything else.

The Plan B frame, in one breath

  • Test first. Don’t guess from a gene or a symptom — check what’s actually happening in the blood, then act on that.
  • Food-first while you test. A calm, anti-inflammatory way of eating is the foundation that runs the whole time — it costs nothing and helps from day one.
  • This is parent education, not medical advice. No doses here on purpose — the specifics belong to your clinician, anchored to your child’s results. Bring this to your team as questions, not instructions.
