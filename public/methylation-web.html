Field Guide · Deconstructed

The methylation web.
You can’t read one cycle alone.

Everyone fixates on methylation — the MTHFR result, the one homocysteine number. But methylation is one cycle in a connected web. It hands off to the folate cycle, drains into transsulfuration, feeds the neurotransmitter and energy cycles, and ties into how the body clears ammonia and makes its master antioxidant. Pull any single gear out and read it alone and you’ll be wrong as often as right. Here’s the whole engine — the cycles, how they connect, and the marker that reads each one.

A “normal” methylation panel can sit right on top of a jammed cycle next door. That’s why Plan B reads the whole web, not one gear.

The core idea, in plain words

Strip away the jargon and methylation is a small loop. The body takes an amino acid called homocysteine and turns it back into methionine, which becomes SAMe — the body’s universal “methyl donor.” SAMe is the molecule that carries the little chemical tag (a methyl group) the body sticks onto things to switch them on or off: it methylates your DNA, your neurotransmitters, and your hormones. Once SAMe gives its tag away it becomes SAH, and the cycle starts over.

The markers that read this loop

Homocysteine, B12, folate, B6, MMA (methylmalonic acid), whole-blood histamine (Walsh’s over- vs under-methylation marker), and ceruloplasmin (the copper layer that rides alongside it). These are the everyday read on whether the methylation loop is turning. See the full how-to-test packet on the Methylation & Detox page.

But here’s the part almost no one explains: that little loop doesn’t run in isolation. The molecule at its hinge — homocysteine — sits at a crossroads.

The crossroads

Picture homocysteine standing at a junction with several roads leading out. It can be re-methylated back to methionine (the methylation loop, fed by folate and B12). It can drain downward into a whole other cycle — transsulfuration — on its way to becoming glutathione and taurine. Or, if the cofactors or the genes aren’t cooperating, it can back up and pool. Each of those roads is its own cycle, with its own genes, cofactors, and markers.

So when you read “homocysteine is high,” you haven’t found an answer — you’ve found a fork in the road. High could mean the methylation loop is stalling, or the drain into transsulfuration is blocked, or B6 (which runs that drain) is missing. The number alone can’t tell you which. That’s the whole reason you can’t read one cycle alone.

The connected cycles

Seven cycles make up the web. Each gets a plain card below, plus the marker that reads it. They share substrate and cofactors — so think of these less as separate machines and more as seven gears in one engine.

1

The folate cycle

This is the cycle that feeds methyl groups in. Folate from food (or supplements) gets converted, step by step, into the active form that hands a methyl group to the methylation loop so homocysteine can become methionine again. MTHFR lives here — the famous gene that, when slow, makes that final activation step sluggish, so less methyl-folate is available to feed the loop next door.

Reads it: RBC + serum folate, MTHFR SNPs, FRAT (brain-folate antibodies), homocysteine

2

The methylation / methionine cycle — the SAMe hub

The center of gravity. Methionine becomes SAMe, SAMe donates its methyl tag to DNA, neurotransmitters, and hormones and becomes SAH, SAH becomes homocysteine, and folate + B12 carry it back to methionine. This is the hub the rest of the web is named after — but it’s still just one loop, and it can only turn as fast as the cycles feeding and draining it allow.

Reads it: homocysteine, B12, MMA, whole-blood histamine, SAMe:SAH ratio

3

Transsulfuration — the drain to glutathione

Instead of recycling, homocysteine can drain down and out here — converting toward glutathione (the body’s master detoxifier and antioxidant) and taurine. CBS lives at the top of this cycle — the gate that pulls homocysteine down the drain. This is the layer a homozygous-CBS child most needs watched: a fast or dysregulated CBS changes how much homocysteine drains, how much sulfate and ammonia get generated downstream, and how gently methyl donors should be pushed.

Reads it: urine amino acids (cysteine, taurine, sulfate), CBS SNPs, B6/P5P

4

The BH4 / neurotransmitter cycle

BH4 (tetrahydrobiopterin) is a little helper molecule the body uses to build dopamine and serotonin — the mood and focus chemicals. It’s wired into the same web: oxidative stress and a backed-up transsulfuration cycle can burn through BH4, and when BH4 runs low, neurotransmitter production stutters. This is one mechanical reason a methylation problem can show up as anxiety, OCD, mood swings, or attention trouble.

Reads it: organic acids (OAT) neurotransmitter metabolites — HVA (dopamine), 5-HIAA (serotonin)

5

The urea cycle — clearing ammonia

The cycle that clears ammonia, the toxic byproduct of breaking down protein. It connects to the web through CBS: when transsulfuration runs hot, it can generate extra ammonia, and if the urea cycle can’t keep up, ammonia overloads. In a child that often looks like irritability, rage, brain fog, or a foggy “off” spell after a high-protein meal. It’s an easy one to miss because no one thinks to check it.

Reads it: plasma ammonia, BUN, urine amino acids (citrulline, ornithine, arginine)

6

The glutathione / antioxidant + detox cycle

This is the cleanup output — where transsulfuration ends up. Glutathione is the body’s master antioxidant and the workhorse of phase-II detox: it neutralizes free radicals and tags toxins for removal. When the web upstream is jammed, glutathione runs low, and the body loses the very tool it needs to clear infection die-off and toxins. The genes here — GSTP1 (glutathione transferases) and SOD2 (superoxide dismutase) — set how well a child recycles and protects this supply.

Reads it: reduced + oxidized glutathione, GSTP1 / SOD2 SNPs, OAT oxidative-stress markers

7

The energy / Krebs cycle — the mitochondria

The energy layer, and the floor under everything else. The Krebs (citric-acid) cycle inside the mitochondria makes ATP — and every cycle above spends ATP to run. Methylation, transsulfuration, the urea cycle: all of it needs energy. So when the mitochondria are stuck in defense mode, the whole web slows down with them — and a backed-up web throws off oxidative stress that hits the mitochondria right back. They feed each other.

Reads it: OAT Krebs-cycle markers, lactate:pyruvate, CoQ10, carnitine — the full menu on the Mitochondria page

The two gears, up close

Methylation ↔ mitochondria: two gears, one engine

That seventh card — the energy floor — isn’t just another cycle. Methylation and the mitochondria are wired into a tight feed-loop, each one building and powering the other. Once you see it, you understand why fatigue and the methylation/mood symptoms always seem to travel together in these kids (and so often in their parents too).

Methylation builds the mitochondria’s machinery

Three of the molecules the mitochondria can’t run without are made using SAMe — the methyl donor from the methylation loop. CoQ10 sits at the core of the energy-production chain. Carnitine hauls fat into the mitochondria to be burned for fuel. And creatine — the cell’s rechargeable energy “battery” — is the single biggest consumer of methyl groups in the whole body (roughly 40%+ of all the SAMe you make goes to making creatine). So a methylation block doesn’t just affect mood and DNA — it can quietly starve the mitochondria of the parts they’re built from.

Mitochondria power methylation back

The loop runs both ways. Making SAMe and running the methylation cycle costs ATP — and ATP is exactly what the mitochondria produce. So the energy plant builds the machinery and the methylation cycle builds the energy plant’s parts. Energy powers methylation; methylation builds the energy machinery. It’s a loop, not a one-way street.

Glutathione is the shared shield

The transsulfuration / CBS arm of the web — the drain off homocysteine — is where the body makes glutathione, its master antioxidant. And glutathione is the main shield protecting the mitochondria from the oxidative damage they create just by running. One web, building the very thing that keeps the energy plant from rusting out.

A flare jams both gears at once

Here’s why this matters in a sick kid. When the mitochondria flip into defense mode — the Cell Danger Response, triggered by infection, mold, or inflammation — it doesn’t just stall energy. It also reshapes methylation, diverting methionine away from the methylation loop and toward defense. One trigger stalls both gears at once. That’s the mechanical reason fatigue and the methylation/mood symptoms travel together — they’re two gears in one engine, and a flare seizes the whole thing.

The cofactors that run all of it

Every gear above turns on the same handful of cofactors — magnesium, zinc, and riboflavin (B2) chief among them (alongside B6/P5P, B12, and folate). Run low on these and several cycles stall at once. It’s a big reason a single deficiency can look like “everything is off” — and why Plan B reads the cofactors as part of the web, not as an afterthought.

The Plan B thesis — one engine, don’t read one gear

These cycles are one connected engine. Folate feeds it, methylation hubs it, transsulfuration drains it to glutathione, the neurotransmitter and urea cycles branch off it, and the mitochondria power all of it. Never read one marker — or one cycle — alone.

Here’s the trap, stated plainly: a “normal” methylation panel can sit right on top of a jammed transsulfuration cycle, a depleted glutathione supply, or an energy cycle stuck in defense mode. The homocysteine looks fine because it’s draining out the back door — straight into a downstream cycle that’s the actual problem. Read methylation in isolation and you’d call that child “clear” and move on. Read the web, and you’d see the gear that’s actually seized.

This is exactly why Plan B looks at the whole engine, not one gear. And the discipline doesn’t change: test first (we don’t guess at a cycle we haven’t measured), food first (the gentlest support before any megadose), and no doses off a webpage — especially here, where pushing methyl donors into a backed-up transsulfuration or ammonia layer can make a sensitive kid worse, not better.

Bottom line

Methylation isn’t a standalone result — it’s one gear in a connected web. Homocysteine sits at a crossroads with roads out to the folate, methionine, transsulfuration, neurotransmitter, urea, glutathione, and energy cycles, and they all share substrate and cofactors. The honest frame: the biochemistry of these cycles is well-established; how aggressively to intervene in any one of them is clinical, individual, and best done low and slow, test-anchored, with your practitioner. The takeaway is simpler than the chemistry: read the whole engine, not one gear. Parent education, not medical advice — bring it to your team as questions.

The practical companion

How to test the whole engine

So how do you actually read a seven-gear web without ordering thirty separate tests? You don’t. The trick is three layers — the blueprint, one workhorse panel, and a few targeted gap-fills — and then reading them against each other.

Layer 1 — Genetics: the blueprint

23andMe raw data → Plan B’s read. One spit kit maps the predispositions across every cycle in the web at once — MTHFR (folate), MTR / MTRR (B12 recycling), CBS (transsulfuration), BHMT (the backup methylation route), COMT (how fast you clear dopamine and estrogen), GSTP1 and SOD2 (glutathione + oxidative defense). It tells you where a child might be weak. It does not tell you whether that weak spot is actually expressing — that’s Layer 2’s job.

Layer 2 — One workhorse functional panel: what’s actually running

A single comprehensive panel reads most of the web in one blood draw + one urine collection. Genova NutrEval or ION Profile + 40 Amino Acids are the two workhorses — each one covers:

  • Organic acids — the mitochondria / energy cycle, neurotransmitter metabolites (the BH4 / dopamine-serotonin layer), and B-vitamin status.
  • Amino acids — the methylation hub, transsulfuration / sulfur handling, and the urea / ammonia layer.
  • Nutrients — CoQ10, carnitine, vitamins, and minerals (the cofactors that run every gear).
  • Oxidative stress / glutathione and homocysteine — the shared-shield and crossroads markers.
Lighter option: the OAT (Organic Acids Test) alone covers the mitochondria + neurotransmitter end if a full NutrEval is too much.

Layer 3 — Targeted gap-fills (same draw)

A handful of specific markers worth adding to the same blood draw to sharpen the picture: homocysteine, MMA, whole-blood histamine, RBC folate, ceruloplasmin, glutathione (reduced + oxidized), CoQ10, carnitine, lactate / pyruvate, ammonia, and the core cofactors — magnesium, zinc, B2, B6. Pick by what the genetics flagged and what the panel left ambiguous.

The integration — read function against genetics

This is the whole discipline, and the part no single test gives you: you read Layer 2 against Layer 1. If a gene predicts a weak cycle and the functional panel confirms that cycle is jammed, you’ve found a real, expressing bottleneck — that’s where to act. If a gene predicts weakness but the function reads normal, the body is compensating — don’t act. Genetics alone tempts you to treat a SNP that isn’t actually causing trouble. Test first; treat the expression, not the prediction. That’s the Plan B discipline.

Honest note: NutrEval, ION Profile, and the OAT are functional-medicine panels — a functional or integrative clinician orders them as a matter of course; a regular PCP can usually order them too, but you’ll need to request them by name. No supplement doses here — those come after the panel, low and slow, with your practitioner.
